# Performance & Reward Holistic Value-Driver Model

## Purpose

This file defines the shared conceptual model used across Performance & Reward knowledge packs, skills, semantic models, dashboard briefs, and diagnostic workflows.

It is the domain-level authority for how the project connects workforce outcomes, reward architecture, performance differentiation, affordability, market positioning, governance, and employee experience. Specialist packs may extend a branch, but they must not redefine shared concepts silently.

This is a project synthesis. It is not presented as a single external provider's proprietary framework.

## Domain objective

Performance & Reward exists to help the organisation attract, retain, motivate, differentiate, and fairly reward the workforce required to deliver its strategy while operating within financial, regulatory, policy, risk, and employee-experience constraints.

A good outcome is not simply higher pay, lower cost, stronger performance ratings, or closer market alignment. It is a controlled balance between:

- strategic workforce capability;
- employee attraction and retention;
- performance and contribution differentiation;
- internal and external fairness;
- affordability and sustainability;
- risk, policy, and regulatory compliance;
- explainability and manager usability;
- operational execution quality.

## Top-level value-driver tree

### 1. Strategic workforce alignment

The reward and performance system supports the workforce capabilities, roles, behaviours, and outcomes required by business strategy.

Key branches:

- critical-role coverage;
- capability and skill availability;
- workforce segmentation;
- job architecture quality;
- career and progression pathways;
- targeted investment in scarce or strategically important work;
- alignment between business priorities, goals, performance assessment, and reward outcomes.

Typical decision questions:

- Are reward investments directed toward the roles and capabilities that matter most?
- Does the job architecture distinguish work of materially different size and complexity?
- Are scarce or critical capabilities visible without creating uncontrolled exceptions?

### 2. Attraction and market competitiveness

The organisation can attract suitable candidates at an acceptable cost and within an acceptable time.

Key branches:

- market positioning by job, level, location, and workforce segment;
- range competitiveness;
- new-hire positioning;
- offer acceptance;
- hiring premiums and exceptions;
- external talent scarcity;
- total-reward competitiveness, not base salary alone.

Typical decision questions:

- Where are hiring outcomes indicating insufficient competitiveness?
- Are new hires systematically positioned above comparable incumbents?
- Are market premiums targeted, temporary, and governed?

### 3. Retention and employee commitment

The organisation retains the people and capabilities it intends to retain while accepting that not all turnover should be prevented.

Key branches:

- regrettable and non-regrettable turnover;
- retention of high performers, critical roles, and scarce skills;
- pay and career-position signals;
- manager and employee experience;
- internal mobility and progression;
- reward-related flight risk;
- concentration of retention interventions.

Typical decision questions:

- Is avoidable turnover concentrated in specific roles, levels, managers, locations, or pay positions?
- Are retention actions addressing a reward issue, a career issue, a management issue, or another cause?
- Are retention payments solving a temporary risk or masking a structural problem?

### 4. Performance and contribution differentiation

Performance processes distinguish contribution in a way that is credible, useful, evidence-based, and connected to consequences.

Key branches:

- goal and expectation quality;
- performance distribution;
- calibration quality;
- consistency across teams and leaders;
- differentiation of fixed and variable reward outcomes;
- treatment of partial-year and special populations;
- conduct, risk, and behavioural adjustments;
- feedback and development quality.

Typical decision questions:

- Do outcomes meaningfully distinguish contribution?
- Are differences attributable to performance, opportunity, structural workforce mix, or manager behaviour?
- Are ratings and reward outcomes calibrated consistently without forcing an artificial distribution?

### 5. Internal fairness and pay equity

Reward outcomes are equitable, explainable, and consistent with legitimate job, performance, market, experience, and location factors.

Key branches:

- equal pay for equal or comparable work;
- unexplained pay gaps;
- range and compa-position differences;
- promotion and hiring outcomes;
- performance-rating and reward-allocation differences;
- intersectional analysis;
- compression and inversion;
- employee-relations and trust implications.

Typical decision questions:

- Which observed differences remain after considering legitimate explanatory factors?
- Are disparities entering through hiring, progression, performance assessment, or reward decisions?
- Are aggregate gaps concealing localised issues?

### 6. Affordability and sustainable investment

Reward investment is financially controlled, directed toward intended outcomes, and sustainable across cycles.

Key branches:

- fixed-reward budget;
- variable-reward pool and funding;
- mandatory and discretionary components;
- cost by workforce segment and organisational hierarchy;
- roll-down and redistribution rules;
- reserves and strategic funds;
- forecast-to-actual variance;
- recurring versus one-off cost;
- currency and international effects.

Typical decision questions:

- Is the organisation within approved budget and risk tolerances?
- What part of the cost is structural and recurring?
- Which allocations are mandatory, discretionary, retained, redistributed, or exceptional?

### 7. Governance, risk, and control

Reward and performance decisions comply with law, regulation, policy, delegated authority, conduct expectations, privacy requirements, and operational controls.

Key branches:

- eligibility and population controls;
- delegated authority;
- policy and framework compliance;
- conduct and risk adjustments;
- exception management;
- audit trail and lineage;
- privacy and sensitive-data access;
- reconciliation to payroll and finance;
- model, calculation, and data-quality controls;
- regulatory and board reporting obligations.

Typical decision questions:

- Can every material outcome be traced to an approved rule, input, decision, and authority?
- Which exceptions require review rather than automatic rejection?
- Are controls preventing errors, detecting errors, or merely reporting them after the event?

### 8. Employee and manager experience

The system is understandable and executable by managers, employees, Reward, HR, payroll, finance, and governance users.

Key branches:

- clarity of policy and guidance;
- manager decision support;
- transparency and explainability;
- process burden;
- timeliness;
- quality of conversations;
- usability of tools and reports;
- confidence in data and calculations;
- dispute and query volumes.

Typical decision questions:

- Can a manager understand what decision is required and why?
- Can an employee understand the basis of an outcome without exposing inappropriate comparative data?
- Are operational problems being mistaken for policy or design problems?

## Shared outcome chain

The domain should generally be analysed through the following chain:

1. **Context and workforce need** — strategy, labour market, organisation design, workforce composition, and business performance.
2. **Architecture and policy** — job structure, ranges, incentive plans, performance frameworks, eligibility, and governance rules.
3. **Inputs and decisions** — employee data, market data, goals, ratings, budgets, manager recommendations, calibrations, and approvals.
4. **Reward and performance outcomes** — salary movements, positioning, ratings, incentive outcomes, promotions, exceptions, and recognition.
5. **Workforce and business consequences** — attraction, retention, motivation, capability, fairness, affordability, risk, and employee experience.

Dashboards must not imply that correlation between a reward input and a workforce outcome proves causation. Diagnostic outputs should distinguish evidence, interpretation, hypothesis, and recommended investigation.

## Cross-domain relationships

The principal relationships are:

- Job architecture defines comparable work and supplies a foundation for ranges, market matching, progression, equity analysis, and workforce segmentation.
- Market competitiveness informs range design and targeted interventions but does not by itself determine individual pay.
- Fixed reward determines recurring pay position and interacts with hiring, retention, equity, compression, and affordability.
- Performance management supplies one input to reward differentiation but must remain distinguishable from reward outcomes.
- Variable reward converts organisation, team, and individual performance inputs into non-fixed outcomes subject to funding, plan, conduct, and governance rules.
- Pay equity tests whether outcomes and processes create unexplained differences across protected or relevant groups.
- Talent and retention analysis evaluates workforce consequences but should not assume pay is the only causal factor.
- Governance and controls apply across every branch rather than operating as a final review step.

## Canonical analytical dimensions

All specialist packs should explicitly consider the following dimensions where relevant:

- person and worker;
- position and job profile;
- job family, discipline, level, and management level;
- employing entity, country, location, and currency;
- organisation, business unit, cost centre, and management hierarchy;
- workforce segment, critical role, and scarce skill;
- employment type, pay group, and eligibility population;
- performance cycle and reward cycle;
- effective date, point-in-time date, and period;
- demographic and equity cohorts subject to privacy controls;
- plan, policy, market survey, benchmark, and range;
- decision owner, approver, exception type, and control status.

## Time and population rules

Every governed metric or diagnostic must declare:

- the population included and excluded;
- the effective or observation date;
- whether it is point-in-time, period activity, cumulative, forecast, or outcome data;
- the organisational hierarchy and job attributes used as of which date;
- treatment of hires, exits, transfers, promotions, leave, partial-year service, and missing records;
- local-currency and reporting-currency treatment;
- suppression and minimum-population rules;
- whether the result is pre-decision, recommended, approved, processed, or paid.

## Required diagnostic discipline

A Performance & Reward diagnostic should progress through four levels:

1. **Describe** — what happened and where.
2. **Compare** — against policy, budget, market, history, peers, ranges, or expected patterns.
3. **Diagnose** — identify plausible structural, process, decision, or data drivers.
4. **Act** — define the decision, owner, intervention, control, or further analysis required.

A dashboard should not jump from description directly to a prescriptive action where alternative explanations remain material.

## Audience applications

### Executives

Emphasise enterprise outcomes, strategic trade-offs, affordability, risk exposure, material exceptions, and decisions requiring executive action.

### HR Business Partners

Emphasise business-unit patterns, workforce implications, manager hotspots, organisational context, and interventions requiring partnership with leaders.

### Reward and Performance specialists

Emphasise definitions, calculations, distributions, policy rules, exceptions, scenario analysis, auditability, and cross-cycle consistency.

### People managers

Emphasise the specific population they are authorised to manage, decision guidance, available budget, employee context, policy boundaries, and required actions.

### Governance, payroll, finance, and HRIS users

Emphasise control totals, reconciliation, exceptions, approvals, processing state, lineage, and unresolved defects.

## Power BI implications

Microsoft or other generic Power BI capabilities may provide implementation techniques. This repository remains authoritative for:

- the business grain;
- domain definitions;
- status and lifecycle distinctions;
- effective-dating rules;
- sensitive-data handling;
- decision and approval states;
- calculation intent;
- diagnostic interpretation;
- required reconciliations and controls.

A technically valid model is not acceptable if it collapses these distinctions or makes an unsupported business inference.

## Governance and anti-patterns

Avoid:

- treating market median as an automatic individual-pay target;
- treating compa-ratio as a complete measure of fairness or competitiveness;
- combining recommendations, approvals, payroll results, and paid outcomes into one status;
- mixing point-in-time employee attributes with period outcomes without an explicit temporal rule;
- interpreting all turnover as undesirable;
- using forced performance distributions as proof of calibration quality;
- publishing small-group or personally identifiable remuneration and performance data;
- presenting aggregate gender or demographic gaps as proof of discrimination or proof of equity;
- assuming a dashboard can replace delegated human judgement;
- allowing generic Power BI guidance to redefine domain logic.

## Open research and design decisions

The following require separate governed decisions or evidence:

- formal taxonomy for strategic workforce segments and critical roles;
- canonical Performance & Reward KPI catalogue;
- standard point-in-time and event-grain model patterns;
- minimum population and suppression thresholds by use case and jurisdiction;
- accepted causal-language standard for retention and performance diagnostics;
- authoritative lifecycle states for recommendations, approvals, processing, and payment;
- the boundary between universal domain definitions and organisation-specific policy rules.
